# Legislative scorecards and behavioral incentives

Research reviewed: November 7, 2026.

Status: research and design recommendations for human review. This document does not
change the approved rubric, assign positions to legislation, or authorize publication.

## Main finding

The strongest approach is a transparent scorecard connected to specific future actions
and credible public recognition or pressure. Evidence is substantially weaker for the
claim that publishing a numerical ranking, by itself, changes legislators' behavior.

For Restore the Fourth, the recommended starting point is a simple policy-alignment
grade alongside separately documented leadership, recent change, consistency, and
evidence coverage. Advance notice and active distribution deserve at least as much
attention as the formula.

This is a focused narrative literature review, not a systematic review or an exhaustive
catalog. It covers individual legislators and related politician-accountability
interventions, rather than institutional ratings of entire legislatures. It emphasizes
primary academic research and scorecard operators' methodology documentation. Operator
documentation establishes how a system works; it does not establish behavioral impact.

## 1. Existing systems: measure the right construct

| System | Measures | Useful features | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LCV National Environmental Scorecard | Agreement with organizational positions on selected votes | Simple percentages, vote-level explanations, historical records | Selection shapes results; agreement does not establish leadership or policy impact |
| National Taxpayers Union ratings | Weighted agreement across a broad set of fiscal votes | Explicit importance weights and broad coverage | Weights are editorial judgments; multiple votes can concern one policy fight |
| Center for Effective Lawmaking: Legislative Effectiveness Scores | Advancement of sponsored legislation through the legislative process | Substantive importance, legislative stages, institutional benchmarks | Legislative success does not establish whether the policy is desirable |
| Lugar–Georgetown Bipartisan Index | Cross-party sponsorship and cosponsorship | Clearly defined cooperation measures, separate from policy alignment | Cooperation can advance either desirable or undesirable policy |
| DW-NOMINATE / Voteview | Ideological positions inferred from roll-call voting | Analysis of voting patterns and ideological context | Ideological position is not a performance grade |

Primary methodology sources:

- [LCV: About the Scorecard](https://www.lcv.org/congressional-scorecard/about-the-scorecard/).
- [NTU: Rates Congress](https://www.ntu.org/ratecongress/).
- [Center for Effective Lawmaking: Methodology](https://thelawmakers.org/methodology).
- [Lugar Center: Bipartisan Index methodology](https://www.thelugarcenter.org/ourwork-44.html).
- [Voteview: DW-NOMINATE scores documentation](https://legacy.voteview.com/dwnomin.htm).

LCV provides a useful communication model for policy alignment. The Center for Effective
Lawmaking provides a useful model for recognizing legislative work beyond final votes.
Its methodology also credits substantial bill language incorporated into another
legislator's bill that becomes law, addressing a weakness of standalone enactment counts.
These are recommendations about fit, not evidence that either system is the most
effective behavioral intervention.

## 2. Direct evidence on politician behavior and accountability

### Grossman and Michelitch (2018): conditional improvements in effort

**Citation:** Guy Grossman and Kristin Michelitch. “Information Dissemination,
Competitive Pressure, and Politician Performance between Elections: A Field Experiment
in Uganda.” *American Political Science Review* 112(2): 280–301.
[Primary paper](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/Grossman_Michelitch_2018.pdf).
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055417000648).

In a multiyear experiment across 20 Ugandan district governments, annual performance
scorecards were produced for incumbents, with constituent dissemination randomized
for selected politicians. Dissemination improved subsequent politician performance
across several measures, but only in competitive constituencies. Service delivery
did not improve.

The comparison identifies the additional effect of constituent dissemination, rather
than the effect of inventing a scorecard where none previously existed. It supports
active distribution under meaningful electoral pressure. It also distinguishes duties
an individual politician can perform from outcomes dependent on multiple institutions.

### Banerjee, Enevoldsen, Pande, and Walton (2024): anticipated disclosure

**Citation:** Abhijit Banerjee, Nils Enevoldsen, Rohini Pande, and Michael Walton.
“Public Information Is an Incentive for Politicians: Experimental Evidence from Delhi
Elections.” *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* 16(3): 323–353.
[Published article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20220088).

Two years before municipal elections, randomly selected Delhi councillors learned
their performance would appear in newspaper report cards before the election.
Treated councillors in high-slum areas increased spending aligned with poorer
residents' preferences. In a cross-cutting experiment, privately supplied actionable
information produced no response. Party and electoral responses also supported the
importance of publicly observable performance.

This is evidence that anticipated public evaluation can change behavior before
publication. It concerns municipal spending decisions, not national legislative
roll-call voting. The result should not be generalized to every councillor or context.

### Humphreys and Weinstein (2012): parliamentary scorecard null results

**Citation:** Macartan Humphreys and Jeremy M. Weinstein. “Policing Politicians:
Citizen Empowerment and Political Accountability in Uganda.” Working paper,
preliminary analysis, March 2012.
[Author-hosted paper](https://macartan.github.io/assets/pdf/papers/2012_policing_politicians.pdf).

A parliamentary scorecard intervention during 2006–2011 varied anticipated and actual
dissemination. The study found no evidence that MPs changed behavior in anticipation
of disclosure or that dissemination changed reelection rates. Survey experiments
showed responsiveness to information, but the field campaigns did not translate that
into durable accountability effects. Constituents often remained poorly informed
about actual performance.

This is an important counterweight to the positive studies. The source is a working
paper, not a peer-reviewed publication; encouraging accounts of an early pilot should
not substitute for its reported larger-study findings.

### Dunning et al. (2019): a coordinated set of information-campaign nulls

**Citation:** Thad Dunning et al. “Voter Information Campaigns and Political
Accountability: Cumulative Findings from a Preregistered Meta-analysis of Coordinated
Trials.” *Science Advances* 5(7): eaaw2612.
[Abstract and results figures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31281891/).
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aaw2612).

Seven randomized trials in six countries, combined using a preregistered analysis,
found no overall evidence that typical nonpartisan voter-information campaigns
changed voter behavior. These interventions do not represent every possible scorecard
design, but the findings substantially weaken a general assumption that information
automatically becomes electoral reward or punishment.

### Charnock (2018): scores embedded in political organizations

**Citation:** Emily J. Charnock. “More Than a Score: Interest Group Ratings and
Polarized Politics.” *Studies in American Political Development* 32(1): 49–78.
[Published article](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0898588X18000056).

Archival evidence shows how ADA and ACA ratings supported electoral strategies and
ideological sorting. The study documents organizational intent and historical
mechanisms, rather than isolating a causal effect of grades on individual votes.

### Synthesis and limits

Credible public evaluation can change some politicians' behavior when relevant
audiences notice and their response matters. Publication alone does not reliably
produce accountability, better public services, or changed roll-call votes.

Three distinct outcomes should remain separate:

1. **Information:** people learn what a politician did.
2. **Selection:** audiences reward or replace politicians based on that record.
3. **Behavior:** an incumbent changes conduct in anticipation of evaluation.

Evidence for one outcome does not establish the others. Positive local-government
experiments outside the United States establish that mechanisms can work; they do not
prove transfer to U.S. congressional surveillance votes.

## 3. Behavioral research: useful mechanisms, uncertain transfer

| Research | Finding or mechanism | Design implication and qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kivetz, Urminsky, and Zheng (2006) | Consumer effort increased as participants approached concrete rewards; an endowed head start accelerated completion | Test attainable intermediate goals and specific next actions. This does not prove arbitrary political grade thresholds motivate legislators. |
| Schultz et al. (2007) | Household energy feedback showed a possible boomerang among already efficient households; explicit approval eliminated the observed boomerang | Recognize strong conduct against a substantive standard. The cleanest randomized comparison concerns added approval messaging; the study had no untreated control arm. |
| Holmström and Milgrom (1991) | Formal multitask incentive theory explains diversion of effort toward rewarded, measurable tasks | Avoid making bill counts or cosponsorship volume substitutes for substantive work. This is theory under specified assumptions, not experimental evidence about legislators. |
| Bevan and Hood (2006) | English health-service target governance exhibited gaming, threshold effects, and neglect of unmeasured activity | Audit whether score improvements reflect real conduct. Institutional case evidence does not establish that all targets fail. |

Primary references:

- Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky, and Yuhuang Zheng (2006). “The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis
  Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention.”
  *Journal of Marketing Research* 43(1): 39–58.
  [Paper](https://s3.amazonaws.com/fieldexperiments-papers2/papers/00658.pdf).
- P. Wesley Schultz et al. (2007). “The Constructive, Destructive, and Reconstructive
  Power of Social Norms.” *Psychological Science* 18(5): 429–434.
  [Paper](https://courses.washington.edu/pbafhall/514/514%20Readings/social%20norms.pdf).
- Bengt Holmström and Paul Milgrom (1991). “Multitask Principal–Agent Analyses:
  Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design.” *Journal of Law, Economics,
  & Organization* 7, special issue: 24–52.
  [Paper](https://web.stanford.edu/~milgrom/publishedarticles/Multitask%20Principal%20Agent.pdf).
- Gwyn Bevan and Christopher Hood (2006). “What's Measured Is What Matters: Targets
  and Gaming in the English Public Health Care System.” *Public Administration*
  84(3): 517–538. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9299.2006.00600.x).

There is no established best presentation format for changing legislators' behavior
among letter grades, bounded percentages, badges, or open-ended rankings. A hard cap
eliminates additional points above the cap by construction; whether politicians stop
improving is a separate empirical question. An open-ended score likewise does not
guarantee a meaningful incentive.

## 4. Measurement cautions

### Vote selection and apparent extremism

James M. Snyder Jr. (1992), “Artificial Extremism in Interest Group Ratings,”
*Legislative Studies Quarterly* 17(3): 319–345, shows how interest-group ratings can
exaggerate ideological extremism and bipolarity.
[Paper](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=3fa98529b21a8c872bcb535e322fa2a0786a95c3&repid=rep1&type=pdf).

A zero or perfect score on selected decisions is not a complete portrait of a
legislator's political position. Publish selection rules and the underlying decisions.

### Agenda changes and historical comparisons

Different Congresses face different decisions. An increase in raw agreement can
reflect easier or different opportunities rather than changed preferences or effort.
Historical grades remain useful, but claims of behavioral improvement need comparable
topics or decisions and explicit qualifications.

### Repeated decisions and thin evidence

Several procedural votes on one legislative fight do not provide the same breadth
of evidence as independent decisions across multiple issues. Report issue coverage
and policy-fight concentration alongside vote counts. Counts are coverage indicators,
not statistical confidence intervals: selected roll calls are not a random sample.

## 5. Recommended design for Restore the Fourth

The following is a research-informed proposal, not a validated optimum or an approved
rubric change.

| Component | Proposed treatment | Question answered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Policy alignment | Transparent percentage on consequential, reviewed votes | Did the member support the preferred policy? |
| Leadership | Separate evidence of sponsorship, amendments, oversight, and advancement | What work did the member undertake beyond voting? |
| Recent change | Current-Congress record alongside historical record | Is recent conduct different? |
| Consistency | Separate presidential-party-context comparison with evidence qualifications | How durable is alignment across observed contexts? |
| Evidence coverage | Decisions, topics, missing records, and available opportunities | How much does the record support the assessment? |
| Next opportunities | Pending actions, deadlines, and announced scoring treatment | What can the office do next? |

Operating principles:

1. **Announce criteria in advance.** Where practical, notify offices before consequential
   decisions, with the preferred action, rationale, and weight.
2. **Make distribution credible.** Identify who will see the assessment, why their
   response matters, and which channels will reach them.
3. **Reward substantive contribution.** Require evidence of contribution and advancement;
   avoid unlimited credit for symbolic activity.
4. **Use stable public standards.** All members should be able to improve simultaneously.
   Relative rankings can add context without defining the grade.
5. **Recognize improvement separately from excellence.** A member can improve while
   retaining a poor historical record. Preserve both facts.
6. **Make corrections reviewable.** Apply consistent evidence rules, retain sources and
   publication history, and provide an accessible correction process.

### Relationship to the current implementation

The implementation reviewed on November 7, 2026 is in
[`src/core/scoring.ts`](../src/core/scoring.ts); its data and experiment workflow are
described in the [agent guide](AGENT-GUIDE.md). These observations describe the inspected
implementation, not a claim about which rubric is approved or deployed.

Useful foundations already include weighted agreement, evidence minimums, optional
recency treatment, and a separate cross-administration classification.

Three issues warrant experiments and human review:

- **Cosponsorship dilution.** Affirmative cosponsorship records enter the weighted
  agreement calculation alongside votes. Depending on weights, inexpensive supportive
  actions can offset consequential opposition. Separating leadership credit would
  expose this tradeoff. Under the documented data model, lack of cosponsorship is not
  an adverse record, making its treatment asymmetric with roll calls.
- **Abstention incentives.** “Not voting” is excluded, creating a potential incentive
  for selective absence. LCV penalizes unexcused missed votes; NTU excludes absences
  and notes relevant limitations. These are different normative choices. Report
  participation separately and define how excused and strategic absences are handled.
- **Context comparability.** Shared-topic checks help, but differences across
  presidential-party contexts do not isolate principle, hypocrisy, or presidential
  influence. Issue mix, decision difficulty, and institutional opportunities can change.

Any scoring experiment must reuse the existing implementation through
[`src/core/experiment.ts`](../src/core/experiment.ts) and `npm run experiment`.
This review does not evaluate current members' grades. Unreviewed staging vote
directions must not be presented as Restore the Fourth's positions.

## 6. Evaluation agenda

Keep public scoring rules identical while testing delivery, subject to a feasible
evaluation design and organizational review. Candidate comparisons include ordinary
publication, direct office briefings, and briefings plus organized local dissemination.

Predefine substantive outcomes: timely support for priority legislation, amendment
support, documented oversight, and consequential votes. Also track symbolic activity,
selective absences, and repeated low-impact actions as possible adverse responses.

Separate reach and comprehension from behavioral impact. Website visits, staff
acknowledgments, and score increases do not alone demonstrate better legislative
conduct or improved privacy protections.

Congressional samples are small, offices influence one another, and available decisions
vary over time. An evaluation needs to address statistical power, spillovers, baseline
differences, and the actual opportunities each member had. Test the intervention against
independent evidence of conduct, not only the metric it rewards.

The highest-priority research question is whether advance notice plus credible
distribution changes consequential behavior beyond ordinary publication. Fine-tuning
letter-grade thresholds should follow evidence about that mechanism.
